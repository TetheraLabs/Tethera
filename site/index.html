What is herding?

When every agent has the same idea at the same second.

A herd is not a conspiracy. It is what happens when thousands of independent programs read the same price, run similar logic, and reach the pool within the same minute.

Shared inputs

Most trading agents on Robinhood Chain watch the same handful of price feeds and the same pools. When ETH moves on a centralised exchange, every one of them sees it within the same second.

Shared logic

Many agents are built on the same frameworks, the same example strategies and the same models. Given the same input, they reach the same conclusion. They do not know about each other, but they behave as if they had agreed.

One pool

A pool has one price. The first agent to arrive gets the price it saw. The hundredth gets a price the first ninety-nine have already moved. Everyone after that is trading against the herd they are part of.

Herd minutes per hour over the last 48 hours: minutes in which fifteen or more automated wallets pushed one pool the same way with almost nobody on the other side. Redrawn every minute from the live count.

Most herds clear in about a minute. About one in ten lasts longer. They are short, sharp and frequent. Herding is not the same as arbitrage: the busiest minutes on the chain are usually two-sided, bots buying and selling the same pool to keep it in line with other markets. That is healthy. A herd is one-sided.

Why it matters

Who pays when the herd runs.

The agents inside it

Each one trades against the others. The price they get is the price the herd made, and the one that arrives last pays most.

The liquidity providers

A one-sided minute is adverse selection by definition: the pool sells into a move it cannot see coming. The busiest minutes are the ones LPs would least like to be quoting in.

Everyone reading the chain

Volume, holders and activity are the numbers people trade on. When a large share of them is bots, or printed by one wallet, every decision built on them is wrong.

How it works

Watch. Count. Charge.

Watch

Every Uniswap v2, v3 and v4 swap on Robinhood Chain is decoded within seconds of the block. Each wallet is labelled by how it behaves over seven days, not by what it claims to be: separate transactions in several pools inside one block, sub-second gaps between transactions, metronomic timing, computed sizes, reaction speed, persistence. Three or more of those and the wallet is automated. One transaction is one trade, so a person's swap routed through three pools never counts as three.

Count

For any pool and direction, the signal returns how many automated wallets are on your side of the trade in the last sixty seconds, how many are on the other side, how many other pools are moving the same way, and where this minute ranks against the pool's own history. One word sums it up: clean, busy, or herd.

Charge

A Uniswap v4 hook reads the public set of automated wallets and counts them per pool, per block. When a herd forms, the pool's fee rises for the herd's side and falls for whoever leans against it. The herd pays for its own impact; Tethera takes a fifth of the surcharge only, never any of the base fee, capped in the contract.

About

Yan, tan, tethera.

One, two, three. Shepherds in Cumbria counted their flocks in a tongue older than English, and a count that still runs on the fells gave this project its name.

Tethera Labs builds market infrastructure for autonomous trading agents, starting on Robinhood Chain. The measurements are free because the market is better when everyone can see the herd. The products earn only when they make a trade better than it would have been. Numbers on this site are measured on mainnet and dated. Nothing here is investment advice, and there is no token.
