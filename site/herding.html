What is herding?

When every agent has the same idea at the same second.

A herd is not a conspiracy. It is what happens when thousands of independent programs read the same price, run similar logic, and reach the pool within the same minute.

Three things make a herd

Shared inputs. Most trading agents on Robinhood Chain watch the same handful of price feeds and the same pools. When ETH moves on a centralised exchange, every one of them sees it within the same second.

Shared logic. Many agents are built on the same frameworks, the same example strategies and the same models. Given the same input, they reach the same conclusion. They do not know about each other, but they behave as if they had agreed.

One pool. A pool has one price. The first agent to arrive gets the price it saw. The hundredth gets a price the first ninety-nine have already moved. Everyone after that is trading against the herd they are part of.

What it looks like on the chain

On 14 October 2026 at 17:04 UTC, 270 automated wallets sold into one ETH/USDG pool inside a single minute. At 17:21, 31 different pools moved the same way at once. On 18 October at 19:47 UTC, 171 automated wallets bought the same ETH/USDG pool in sixty seconds with almost nobody selling. On 15 October at 12:49, a memecoin pool saw 2,848 wallets in one minute, 165 of them software.

Most herds clear in about a minute. About one in ten lasts longer. They are short, sharp and frequent: we count about fifteen hundred one-sided herds a day across the chain, each one a minute in which fifteen or more automated wallets pushed the same pool the same way.

Herding is not the same as arbitrage. The busiest minutes on the chain are usually two-sided: bots buying and selling the same pool to keep it in line with other markets. That is healthy. A herd is one-sided. Everyone wants out, or everyone wants in, and nobody is on the other side of the trade.
